# manual-outreach

**What it is.** The unglamorous but reliable core of link building: personally contacting writers, publications, podcast hosts, and tool companies where it genuinely makes sense to add your link — including the "write a case study for them" variant.

**When to use.**
- A high-authority outlet has previously covered your category and a new angle (a new model, feature, or result) gives them a reason to revisit it.
- You appeared somewhere (podcast, interview, mention) without a link and can simply ask for one.
- You're a happy user of a tool that publishes customer case studies — you can trade a case study for a link.

**Method.**
1. Build an opportunity list from where it naturally fits to add your link. Sources: Ahrefs (who covered your topic before), Google for your category + "review/best," your own mentions and interviews, and tools you actively use.
2. **Revisit-the-topic angle (high-authority outlets):** find a publication that previously wrote about your category, then give them a fresh reason — e.g. a new underlying model dramatically improves your output. Pull the writer's contact (Hunter.io) and draft a low-friction pitch: here's free access / a coupon to try the new thing; no ask to write anything. If the result impresses them, they often write it up unprompted.
3. **Missing-link ask:** when you're featured somewhere without a backlink (a podcast appearance, an interview, a write-up), simply email the host/editor and ask them to add a link to your site. This is the highest-conversion outreach because they've already endorsed you.
4. **Free-access-to-coverers:** when you notice anyone currently writing about your category, reach out offering free product access to try. Some will choose to write about you; many won't — it's volume, but cheap and on-topic.
5. **Case-study trade (lesson 04-09):** identify a tool you genuinely use and like — ideally one that's not huge and has few or no published case studies. Offer to write a positive case study in exchange for a backlink. Their content writer will usually interview you; provide real value; the published case study links back to you. Companies want more happy-customer case studies, so this is a high-yield, mutually beneficial ask.
6. Keep every message personal and specific to that recipient. Draft all outreach for the user to review and send — never auto-send.

**Decision criteria / heuristics.**
- Only reach out where adding your link genuinely makes sense for their content — irrelevant pitches damage your reputation in the niche.
- Lower the ask: "here's free access, no obligation" converts better than "please write about us."
- For case studies, target smaller tools with few existing case studies — far easier to land than a giant like HubSpot.
- Expect this to be hit-and-miss and repetitive; the win comes from doing it consistently and personally, not from one perfect email.

**Example.** The course cases: (1) A high-authority tech outlet had previously covered AI headshots; when a much better image model launched, the team emailed the writer offering a free coupon to try it — no ask — and the writer published a full piece on using the new model in the tool. (2) After a podcast appearance with no link in the show notes, a quick email to the host got a backlink added from a very high-DR site. (3) For case studies, the team told a tool they happily used that they'd write a case study in return for a link; the tool's writer interviewed them and published it with a backlink.

**Pitfalls.**
- Spamming or mass-DMing "I built this, feature me" — it's ineffective and harms your name.
- Pitching outlets where your link wouldn't naturally belong.
- Leading with a heavy ask instead of an easy, value-first offer.
- Auto-sending outreach — every message is drafted for user review and sent by the user.
